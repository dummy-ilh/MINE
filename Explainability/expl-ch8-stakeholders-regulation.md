# Chapter 8 — Explainability in Practice: Stakeholders and Regulation

Chapters 2–7 covered the methods and how to evaluate them. This chapter is about the practical, organizational side: who actually needs an explanation and what kind, what regulation genuinely requires, and a use case for explainability that has nothing to do with an external audience at all — the ML team explaining the model to itself.

## 8.1 Different audiences need genuinely different things from "explainability"

**Why this needs to be said explicitly, rather than assumed obvious:** "explainability" is often discussed as if it's one deliverable, but the right explanation for one audience can be actively unhelpful for another — building a single, one-size-fits-all "explanation artifact" and handing it to every stakeholder is a common practical mistake.

- **A data scientist debugging a model** wants a detailed, technical, potentially messy explanation — full SHAP interaction values (Chapter 5 of your Feature Importance syllabus), raw saliency maps despite their known noise (Chapter 4, §4.2), attention patterns across every layer — because the goal is finding a bug or a spurious pattern, and more raw detail, even imperfect detail, is useful for that specific job.
- **A regulator/auditor** wants a rigorous, verifiable, complete accounting — this is exactly where SHAP's efficiency axiom (Chapter 5 of your Feature Importance syllabus, contributions summing exactly to the prediction) and faithfulness-tested methods (Chapter 7 of this syllabus) matter most, since an auditor's job is specifically to check that nothing is hidden or hand-waved.
- **An end user affected by a decision** wants something short, actionable, and in plain language — this is squarely where counterfactual explanations (Chapter 3) shine over a raw attribution table, since "here's what you could change" is directly usable in a way "here are twelve SHAP values" typically isn't for a non-technical person.

**The practical takeaway:** a mature explainability system for a high-stakes model typically needs to produce **multiple, audience-specific explanation artifacts** from the same underlying analysis, rather than one artifact serving every purpose — and picking the wrong format for a given audience (a raw SHAP dump handed to an affected end user, say) can genuinely undermine the goal of explainability even when the underlying explanation is technically sound.

## 8.2 GDPR's "right to explanation" — what it actually requires, at a high level

**The relevant provisions, briefly:** the EU's General Data Protection Regulation includes provisions (particularly Article 22, along with related recitals) addressing automated individual decision-making, giving individuals certain rights when a decision "based solely on automated processing" produces "legal effects" or "similarly significantly affects" them — including, under some readings, a right to obtain "meaningful information about the logic involved."

**Why "what it actually requires" is a genuinely contested legal question, not a settled technical checklist:** there's real, ongoing legal and academic debate about how strong this "right to explanation" actually is in practice — some scholars argue the GDPR text supports a fairly robust individual right to a substantive explanation of automated decisions; others argue the actual binding legal text is considerably weaker and more limited than the popular "right to explanation" framing suggests, with the stronger requirements living more in non-binding recitals than in the enforceable articles themselves. **The honest, interview-ready framing is to acknowledge this genuine ambiguity rather than presenting a confident, oversimplified summary of what the law requires** — a stronger answer says "there's real debate about how far this right extends" than one that states a specific, confident interpretation as settled fact.

**What's less disputed, and safe to state directly:** organizations deploying automated decision-making at meaningful scale, in domains GDPR covers, are expected to be able to provide **some** account of the logic behind a decision, particularly for decisions with significant effects on individuals (credit, employment, insurance) — which is exactly why the explanation methods in this syllabus (and the documentation practices in §8.3) matter as practical compliance capabilities, regardless of exactly how the strongest legal interpretation is eventually settled.

## 8.3 Connecting back to Model Cards and Datasheets

**Why this connection matters, and shouldn't be treated as two separate topics:** your Fairness & Responsible AI prep covered Model Cards (documenting a trained model's intended use, evaluation metrics, and known limitations) and Datasheets for Datasets (documenting the data's provenance and collection process) as the **documentation layer** that makes fairness findings auditable and reviewable rather than implicit. Explainability findings belong in exactly the same documentation layer, not a separate one — a Model Card's "ethical considerations" and "caveats" sections are precisely where you'd record, for instance, "SHAP-based global feature importance shows X, Y, Z as the dominant drivers; a randomization sanity check (Chapter 6 of this syllabus) confirmed the saliency method used for the vision component is faithful; counterfactual analysis shows recourse is available via [these levers] for denied applicants."

**Why documenting explainability findings, not just generating them once, matters practically:** an explanation generated once during model development and never revisited becomes stale exactly the way an undocumented fairness metric does (your Fairness prep's governance chapter covered this same principle for fairness specifically) — a model retrained on new data, or deployed to a new population, can have its explanation profile shift meaningfully, and a versioned, documented explanation (tied to a specific model version, the same versioning discipline from your Fairness prep's Chapter 9) is what lets someone downstream know whether a given explanation is still trustworthy for the currently-deployed model, or represents an earlier version's behavior.

## 8.4 Explainability as an internal debugging tool — a genuinely distinct use case

**Why this deserves its own section, not just a mention:** everything discussed so far in this chapter assumes explainability's purpose is communicating to an external or semi-external audience (a regulator, an end user, even a separate compliance team). But explainability methods are also, independently, one of the ML team's own most valuable **debugging tools** — used to find problems in the model itself, with no external audience involved at all.

**Concrete debugging use cases:**
- **Finding spurious correlations:** a SHAP or saliency-based explanation revealing that a model relies heavily on an unexpected, likely-spurious feature (a background watermark in training images, a data-collection-site identifier accidentally correlated with the label) is a strong, actionable signal of a **shortcut** the model learned instead of the genuine pattern you intended it to learn.
- **Detecting data leakage:** if an explanation shows a model relying overwhelmingly on a single feature with suspiciously high, almost-too-good predictive power, that's often the first visible symptom of a leaked feature (one that encodes the target directly or near-directly, the exact leakage failure mode covered in your Feature Selection syllabus's Chapter 8) — explanation methods frequently surface this kind of bug well before a careful manual data audit would.
- **Shortcut learning more generally:** a model can achieve strong benchmark performance while relying on an entirely different signal than the one a human would use for the same task (the classic example: a skin-lesion classifier partly relying on the presence of a ruler in the image, since rulers were more often included alongside malignant lesions in the training data, for reasons unrelated to the lesion itself) — explanation methods, particularly ones covered in Chapters 4–6 for image/text data, are frequently the tool that first surfaces this kind of problem, well before it's caught any other way.

**Why this use case has a different bar for "trustworthy enough" than the external-facing use cases in §8.1:** for internal debugging, a somewhat noisy or imperfect explanation method (even one that fails part of the Chapter 7 faithfulness testing) can still be genuinely useful as a **hypothesis-generation tool** — a saliency map suggesting "maybe the model is looking at the ruler, not the lesion" is worth investigating further even if that saliency method wouldn't pass a rigorous faithfulness audit, precisely because the cost of a false lead here (a wasted investigation) is much lower than the cost of an unfaithful explanation being presented as ground truth to a regulator or an affected individual. Matching the rigor of your explanation method to the actual stakes of how it's being used is itself a key practical skill this whole topic is building toward.
