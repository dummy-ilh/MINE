# Chapter 6 — Explainability for Specific Modalities

Chapters 4–5 covered specific methods (saliency, Integrated Gradients, Grad-CAM, attention, TCAV). This chapter steps back and asks a different question: what makes explanation genuinely *harder* for some data modalities than others, and what are the modality-specific failure modes worth knowing about — including one of the most important cautionary results in this whole topic, the saliency sanity-check findings.

## 6.1 NLP: why explaining text is structurally harder than tabular data

**The core difficulty, stated precisely:** in tabular data, a feature is a clean, independent column — perturbing or attributing importance to "income" is a well-defined operation. In text, the "features" are tokens, and tokens **interact non-locally and non-additively** in a way tabular features typically don't — the meaning of a word depends heavily on other words that can be far away in the sequence (long-range dependencies), and swapping one word for a synonym can leave the meaning nearly unchanged while swapping a seemingly-similar word can flip the meaning entirely (negation, sarcasm, idiom).

**Why naive per-token attribution can mislead as a result:** a per-token saliency or attribution score (highlighting "these words mattered most") can miss that the *relationship between* two distant tokens — not either token individually — is what actually drove the prediction. E.g., in the sentence "The movie was not really all that great," per-token attribution might highlight "great" as strongly positive in isolation, while the actual sentiment-determining relationship is between "not" (far earlier in the sentence) and "great" — a genuine interaction effect that a naive, position-by-position highlighting scheme doesn't naturally surface, even though methods like Integrated Gradients (Chapter 4) can, in principle, still assign appropriate credit if applied carefully, since they measure sensitivity to the whole input, not just isolated positions.

**Why paraphrase specifically breaks naive approaches:** two sentences with nearly identical meaning but completely different surface tokens ("the movie was great" vs. "I really enjoyed the film") would receive completely different token-level attribution maps despite the model (ideally) treating them near-identically at the semantic level — a token-level explanation is tied to the specific surface form, not the underlying meaning the model is actually reasoning about, which is a modality-specific limitation that doesn't have a clean tabular-data analogue.

## 6.2 Vision: the sanity-check literature — a critical, commonly-cited failure mode

This is one of the single most important results to know cold for this topic, because it's a direct, empirical challenge to whether popular saliency methods are measuring anything about the model at all.

**The finding (Adebayo et al., 2018, "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps"):** the researchers proposed a simple, devastating test — take a trained model and a saliency method, generate a saliency map, then **progressively randomize the model's weights** (e.g., starting from the last layer and working backward, or randomizing the whole network) and check whether the saliency map changes. **The logic:** if a saliency method is genuinely explaining what the *trained* model learned, its output should change substantially once you destroy that learned structure by randomizing the weights — a saliency map that stays nearly identical on a randomized, untrained network as it was on the real, trained network cannot possibly be reflecting anything the model actually learned, since a randomly-weighted network hasn't learned anything at all.

**The result:** several popular saliency methods **failed this test** — producing visually similar-looking saliency maps regardless of whether the network's weights were the real, trained weights or completely randomized ones. This means those specific methods' outputs were being driven substantially by the *architecture and input structure* alone (e.g., edge-detection-like patterns that any convolutional structure would produce, trained or not), rather than by anything the model actually learned during training — a strong, direct piece of evidence that a saliency map "looking reasonable" to a human is not, by itself, any guarantee that it reflects genuine model behavior.

**Why this matters as the central cautionary tale for this entire chapter, not just a vision-specific footnote:** this result directly validates Chapter 1's concern about post-hoc explanations of black boxes being potentially misleading (§1.2) — it's not an abstract worry, it's an empirically demonstrated failure, with a simple, replicable test anyone can run. **The practical takeaway:** before trusting a saliency method's output for anything important, run (or ask whether someone has run) exactly this randomization sanity check — a method that passes it (its output changes substantially under weight randomization) has cleared a low but meaningful bar; a method that fails it should not be trusted regardless of how visually compelling its output looks.

## 6.3 Time series: the extra difficulty of temporal attribution

**Why time series adds a distinct difficulty beyond both tabular and NLP challenges:** a time series prediction typically depends not just on individual time-point values, but on **patterns across time** — a trend, a sudden spike, a periodic cycle, the *rate of change* rather than any single absolute value. Attributing importance to "this one timestep" the way you'd attribute importance to "this one tabular feature" often misses the point, since the informative signal frequently lives in the *shape* of a sequence of values, not any individual point within it.

**Concrete illustration:** suppose a model predicts equipment failure from sensor readings, and the actual predictive signal is "temperature has been steadily rising over the last 10 readings" (a trend) rather than any single reading being unusually high in isolation. A naive per-timestep attribution method might spread modest-looking importance across all 10 timesteps individually, or highlight just the single most recent (and therefore highest) reading — neither of which correctly communicates that it's the **trend across the whole window**, not any individual point, that's actually driving the prediction.

**What this implies practically:** temporal explanation methods generally need to attribute importance to **windows, patterns, or derived features (rate of change, moving averages) explicitly constructed to capture the temporal structure**, rather than naively applying a tabular-style per-timestep attribution method and hoping it happens to surface trend-level patterns — this is an active area without as clean a consensus "best method" as tabular attribution (SHAP) or vision attribution (Integrated Gradients/Grad-CAM) currently have.

## 6.4 The unifying lesson across all three modalities

Each modality's specific difficulty traces back to the same underlying issue, worth stating explicitly as the chapter's takeaway: **explanation methods are easiest to trust when the unit of attribution (a tabular feature, a pixel, a token, a timestep) genuinely corresponds to an independent, meaningful unit of the model's actual reasoning.** Tabular features often do (though even there, correlated features complicate things, per your Feature Importance syllabus's Chapter 9). Individual pixels, tokens, and timesteps often *don't* — the real signal frequently lives in relationships, patterns, and interactions that span multiple such units, and naive per-unit attribution can miss or misrepresent exactly the thing that mattered. This is precisely why methods that account for structure (Integrated Gradients' path-integration, Grad-CAM's use of semantically-richer later-layer activations, TCAV's concept-level testing) tend to outperform naive per-unit approaches for these harder modalities.
