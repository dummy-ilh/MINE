# Chapter 10 — Practical Synthesis

This is the last chapter, and like the synthesis chapters in your other prep topics, it's a rehearsal rather than new material. We'll take one scenario — explaining a specific denied loan application — end-to-end through global importance, a local SHAP explanation, and two deliberate diagnostic catches, then close with a decision framework and practice interview questions.

## 10.1 End-to-end worked case: explaining one denied loan application

**Setup:** you have a trained gradient-boosted tree model predicting loan approval, and a specific applicant — call them Applicant X — was denied. A stakeholder (a compliance reviewer, or the applicant themselves) asks: "why was this specific application denied?"

**Step 1 — Start with the global picture, but don't stop there (Chapter 8, §8.1).** Pull up the model's global feature importance to orient yourself: which features matter most, on average, across the whole population? Suppose gain-based importance (Chapter 2, §2.3) ranks `credit_score`, `debt_to_income`, and `employer_industry_code` as the top three. This is useful context, but it answers "what matters in general," not "what mattered for Applicant X specifically" (Chapter 1, §1.2) — you need to go local next.

**Step 2 — Catch the cardinality-bias issue before trusting that global ranking fully (Chapter 2, §2.4; Chapter 9, §9.2).** `employer_industry_code` has 200 distinct categories — exactly the kind of high-cardinality feature MDI/gain-style importance is known to inflate. Before including it in your explanation with full confidence, cross-check with permutation importance (Chapter 4) computed on a genuinely held-out set (Chapter 9, §9.3). Suppose this reveals `employer_industry_code`'s true importance is meaningfully lower than the gain-based ranking suggested — you'd flag this discrepancy rather than reporting the inflated number as fact.

**Step 3 — Compute a local SHAP explanation for Applicant X specifically (Chapter 5).** Using TreeSHAP (exact and fast for this gradient-boosted model), compute per-feature SHAP values for this one prediction. Suppose the result shows: baseline approval probability 70%, `debt_to_income` contributed −25 percentage points (Applicant X's debt-to-income ratio is unusually high), `credit_score` contributed −8 points, and `employer_industry_code` contributed only −2 points despite its inflated-looking global rank — confirming the Step 2 suspicion that this feature wasn't actually a meaningful driver for this specific applicant, whatever its global ranking suggested. Recall from Chapter 5's efficiency axiom (§5.3) that these contributions plus the baseline sum exactly to the model's actual predicted probability for Applicant X — nothing is left unaccounted for.

**Step 4 — Check for correlated-feature distortion before finalizing the explanation (Chapter 9, §9.1).** Suppose `debt_to_income` is correlated with another feature in the model, `monthly_expenses`. Before reporting `debt_to_income` as the dominant driver, a quick VIF check (Chapter 3, §3.3) or a look at grouped SHAP behavior confirms these two aren't so redundant that the credit is arbitrarily split between them — the explanation is genuinely attributing to the right feature, not an artifact of which correlated partner happened to "win."

**Step 5 — Produce the final, defensible explanation.** *"Applicant X's application was denied primarily due to a high debt-to-income ratio, which alone reduced their approval probability by roughly 25 percentage points relative to the model's baseline; a below-average credit score contributed a further 8-point reduction. Their employer industry code, despite appearing prominently in the model's overall (gain-based) feature ranking, contributed only marginally to this specific decision — a discrepancy explained by that feature's high cardinality inflating its global importance score, confirmed via a held-out permutation-importance check."* This is a stronger, more honest explanation than "the model's top global feature is X, so that's probably why" — it's local, quantified, axiom-guaranteed to sum correctly, and explicitly accounts for a known bias rather than silently propagating it.

## 10.2 A general decision framework

1. **Start global for orientation, go local for the actual explanation.** A global ranking tells you what the model relies on in general; a specific prediction needs a local method (SHAP, or LIME/Anchors if SHAP's compute cost is prohibitive) (Chapter 1, §1.2; Chapter 8).
2. **Match the importance method to the model type first.** TreeSHAP for trees, DeepSHAP for neural nets, standardized coefficients/odds ratios for linear/logistic models, KernelSHAP or LIME/Anchors only when no model-specific shortcut exists (Chapters 2, 3, 5, 6).
3. **Never trust a training-data-only importance measure (MDI, boosted-tree "weight") at face value if cardinality varies across your feature set** — cross-check with a held-out-validated method (permutation importance, SHAP) whenever this applies (Chapters 2, 9).
4. **Check for correlated features before interpreting any ranking**, and identify which specific mechanism (splitting, masking, or extrapolation) is at play if something looks off, since the fix differs by mechanism (Chapter 9, §9.1).
5. **Check ranking stability** across a resample or two before treating any single ranking as final, especially if you suspect redundant features (Chapter 8, §8.4).
6. **If the downstream use is an intervention, not just an explanation, say so explicitly and flag that causal methods — not a better importance method — are what's actually needed** (Chapter 9, §9.4).

## 10.3 Practice interview questions with strong answer structures

**"Why do MDI and permutation importance disagree on this model?"**
Tests whether you understand the mechanism, not just the fact of disagreement. Strong answer: name the specific likely cause — most commonly, a cardinality mismatch across features (MDI/gain inflate high-cardinality features because they have more candidate splits to potentially get lucky on, with no held-out check; permutation importance validates against held-out data and isn't fooled the same way) (Chapter 2, §2.4; Chapter 9, §9.2) — then note you'd trust permutation importance (or SHAP) more in that specific situation, and explain why with the mechanism, not just an appeal to authority ("permutation importance is just better").

**"How would you explain a single denied loan application to a regulator?"**
Structure exactly like 10.1: local SHAP explanation (not a global ranking, which answers the wrong question for this use case), explicitly checking for and flagging any known biases (cardinality) or correlated-feature distortions before finalizing, and grounding the explanation in SHAP's efficiency guarantee (the contributions sum exactly to the actual prediction, so nothing is hand-waved or left out) — a regulator will specifically want to know the explanation is complete and verifiable, not just plausible-sounding.

**"A stakeholder says a feature 'obviously' matters, but your importance ranking says it doesn't. What do you check?"**
Run the Chapter 9 checklist explicitly, in order: held-out validation (is this a training-only measure like MDI being fooled?), cardinality mismatch, correlated features (is this feature's signal being masked by a correlated partner?), ranking stability across resamples, and finally — if all of that checks out and the feature genuinely doesn't help prediction — consider whether the stakeholder is actually making a causal claim ("this factor really does drive outcomes in the real world") rather than a predictive one, since those are different, both-legitimate questions that a predictive importance ranking simply cannot resolve on its own (Chapter 9, §9.4).

**"What's the difference between a PDP and an ICE plot, and when would a PDP alone mislead you?"**
Give the definitions directly (Chapter 7, §7.1 and §7.3), then the concrete heterogeneity example: a PDP averages across the whole dataset and can look flat even when a feature has strong, opposite-direction effects in different subgroups that happen to cancel out on average — ICE plots reveal this by showing individual curves instead of one averaged curve, and should be treated as a routine companion to any PDP rather than an optional extra.

**"Explain why SHAP is considered more principled than LIME."**
Ground this specifically in the four axioms (Chapter 5, §5.3) — efficiency, symmetry, dummy, additivity — and the uniqueness theorem they imply, versus LIME's dependence on several practitioner choices (perturbation strategy, kernel width, surrogate model family) that have no single canonically correct answer and can produce genuinely different explanations for the same prediction (Chapter 6, §6.2). Close by noting SHAP isn't strictly superior in every practical sense — it's more expensive to compute in general (only TreeSHAP and DeepSHAP get around this cheaply) and LIME/Anchors remain useful when compute is constrained or no efficient SHAP variant exists for the model type.
